# Mortartium Field Map

Mortartium studies how cultures transform death, remains, bodily matter, ruin, and destructive material processes into art, architecture, ritual authority, and transmissible memory.

The project does not begin by asking whether one remembered claim is true. It begins by mapping the field in which such claims become thinkable, testable, symbolic, material, institutional, or false.

## Core research question

How do funerary rites, remains, destructive transformation, and artistic media interact across architecture, sacred craft, fine art, civic monument, restoration, and esoteric interpretation?

## Step-by-step taxonomy of the field

The field should be taught as a sequence. Each step asks a different question.

### Step 1 — Rite

What death-practice or transition-practice is involved?

Examples:

- Burial.
- Cremation.
- Mummification.
- Relic translation.
- Ossuary collection.
- Excarnation.
- Foundation sacrifice.
- Martyr cult.
- Ancestor rite.
- Tomb visitation.
- Funerary feasting.
- Consecration.
- Restoration or reburial.

Teaching point: before reading symbols or materials, identify the rite or death-practice field.

### Step 2 — Remain or substrate

What matter is being transformed or preserved?

Examples:

- Human bone, ash, hair, blood, skin, teeth, organs, relic fragments.
- Animal bone, hide, parchment, vellum, horn, shell, ivory, gut, glue.
- Plant matter, wood, charcoal, textile fiber, dye plants, paper.
- Mineral matter, clay, lime, stone, sand, glass, metal, pigment, salt, plaster, mortar.
- Civilizational remains: ruins, spolia, broken monuments, inscriptions, tomb fragments.

Teaching point: “remains” is broader than human remains. The field includes bodily remains, animal remains, plant remains, mineral residues, and cultural ruins.

### Step 3 — Destructive transformation

What process destroys the old form and makes the new form possible?

Examples:

- Burning.
- Calcination.
- Slaking.
- Firing.
- Vitrification.
- Grinding.
- Dissolution.
- Fermentation.
- Putrefaction.
- Scraping.
- Stretching.
- Cutting.
- Hollowing.
- Polishing.
- Binding.
- Inscribing.
- Illumination.

Teaching point: art is often not creation from nothing. It is controlled destruction and recomposition.

### Step 4 — Medium or technique

Which art or craft medium receives the transformed matter?

Examples:

- Mortar.
- Brick.
- Stained glass.
- Pigment.
- Fresco.
- Mosaic.
- Parchment manuscript.
- Reliquary.
- Sarcophagus.
- Column.
- Crypt.
- Ossuary.
- Textile.
- Drum.
- Flute.
- Mask.
- Icon.
- Plaster.
- Concrete.
- Ceramic.
- Metal casting.

Teaching point: the same remains-process may enter many media. Bone can become relic, pigment, ash, instrument, ornament, or memorial medium.

### Step 5 — Form

What stable artifact or architectural form results?

Examples:

- Tomb.
- Grave marker.
- Column.
- Cippus.
- Altar.
- Crypt.
- Cathedral.
- Chapel.
- Wall.
- Brick road.
- Foundation.
- Rose window.
- Manuscript.
- Icon.
- Ossuary.
- Reliquary.
- Monument.
- Ruin.
- Restored surface.

Teaching point: forms are not neutral containers. A column, altar, crypt, window, or manuscript changes how the transformed matter is read.

### Step 6 — Symbolic operation

What does the form do symbolically?

Examples:

- Death-bind.
- Light-body.
- Relic-core.
- Royal furnace.
- Inscribed skin.
- Bone-to-color.
- Body-as-architecture.
- Ruin-as-body.
- Grave-as-axis.
- Blood-earth.
- Salt confession.
- Threshold descent.
- Resurrection.
- Containment.
- Substitution.
- Sacrifice.
- Civic apotheosis.
- Ancestor transmission.

Teaching point: the symbolic layer is real as interpretation, but it must not be confused with material recipe.

### Step 7 — Institution and authority

Who authorizes, controls, interprets, or preserves the transformation?

Examples:

- Family.
- Priesthood.
- Temple workshop.
- Cathedral chapter.
- Guild.
- Mason.
- Scribe.
- Monarch.
- Senate.
- State.
- Museum.
- Conservator.
- Archaeologist.
- Esoteric order.
- Local community.
- Restorer.

Teaching point: remains and transformed matter become public meaning through authority structures.

### Step 8 — Reception and restoration

How is the object later read, damaged, repaired, moved, restored, or mythologized?

Examples:

- Relic translation.
- Tomb opening.
- Museum relocation.
- Capitol column relocation.
- Cathedral restoration.
- Repointing.
- Over-restoration.
- Ruin romanticization.
- Esoteric reinterpretation.
- National memory.
- Tourism.
- Hoax, legend, or conspiracy reception.

Teaching point: later reception is part of the artifact’s life, but not necessarily evidence of original intention.

## How examples should be taught

Every example should move through the taxonomy in order:

1. Rite.
2. Remain or substrate.
3. Destructive transformation.
4. Medium or technique.
5. Form.
6. Symbolic operation.
7. Institution and authority.
8. Reception and restoration.
9. Evidence and claim boundary.

## Example: parchment manuscript

1. Rite / context: sacred, legal, monastic, scholarly, or administrative transmission.
2. Remain/substrate: animal skin.
3. Destructive transformation: flaying, liming, scraping, stretching, drying, smoothing.
4. Medium: parchment or vellum.
5. Form: manuscript, codex, scroll, charter.
6. Symbolic operation: inscribed skin; flesh becomes memory or law.
7. Authority: scribe, monastery, court, church, archive.
8. Reception/restoration: conservation, cataloging, digitization.
9. Boundary: ordinary parchment facts do not prove human remains or hidden ritual intent.

## Example: lime mortar joint

1. Rite / context: construction, repair, restoration, architectural binding.
2. Remain/substrate: limestone, sand, water, possible ash or crushed brick.
3. Destructive transformation: calcination, slaking, mixing, carbonation.
4. Medium: lime mortar.
5. Form: joint between stones or bricks.
6. Symbolic operation: death-bind, covenant, repairable joint.
7. Authority: mason, builder, conservator, restoration regime.
8. Reception/restoration: repointing, decay, efflorescence, spalling, conservation ethics.
9. Boundary: symbolic death-bind is not strict etymology and not proof of esoteric intent.

## Example: column-tomb lane

1. Rite / context: burial, cremation, imperial memory, funerary monument.
2. Remain/substrate: ash, urn, stone, inscription, base, column.
3. Destructive transformation: cremation, quarrying, carving, inscription.
4. Medium: column, cippus, altar, sarcophagus, crypt support.
5. Form: vertical memorial or grave architecture.
6. Symbolic operation: grave-as-axis, ascent, civic apotheosis, public memory.
7. Authority: family, state, senate, priesthood, museum, heritage authority.
8. Reception/restoration: ruin survival, relocation, national memory, esoteric rereading.
9. Boundary: a column may be symbolically funerary without containing a full body.

## Example: horror of bricks lane

1. Rite / context: currently unknown; possible labor, sacrifice, kiln, or esoteric context.
2. Remain/substrate: clay, sand, iron, lime, water, firing atmosphere; possible remembered blood/body motif.
3. Destructive transformation: digging, mixing, molding, drying, firing.
4. Medium: brick.
5. Form: wall, road, kiln, building, prison, temple, grave structure.
6. Symbolic operation: royal furnace, blood-earth, fired body, forced transformation.
7. Authority: brickmaker, mason, state, patron, empire, forced labor regime, esoteric author.
8. Reception/restoration: legend, horror motif, labor memory, material science.
9. Boundary: red brick does not imply blood. The motif is a research seed.

## Repository consequence

Mortartium must not become a pile of disconnected artifact cards. It must teach the step-by-step taxonomy of the field, then place examples inside that taxonomy.

Artifacts illustrate the field. They do not define it.
