# Form Taxonomy

The form taxonomy answers one question: what stable artifact or architectural shape organizes transformed matter into cultural function?

A medium becomes culturally legible through form. Bone as relic, pigment, flute, ossuary wall, or museum specimen is not the same object. Form changes the field of meaning.

## How to read a form

For each form, ask:

1. What medium or substrate does it organize?
2. What rite or practice field surrounds it?
3. What destructive transformation produced the medium?
4. What symbolic operation does the form perform?
5. Which institution authorizes or preserves it?
6. What examples can teach it?
7. What evidence is required?
8. What must not be inferred?

## 1. Burial and containment forms

### Grave

**Function:** basic burial locus; marks the dead in ground, stone, memory, or map.

**Common substrates:** corpse, coffin, shroud, soil, stone, marker, flowers, offerings.

**Symbolic operations:** containment, return to earth, ancestor place, name-location binding.

**Examples to source later:** cemetery graves, Quaker burial grounds, road necropoleis, family plots.

**Boundary:** grave presence does not imply esoteric coding.

### Tomb / mausoleum

**Function:** constructed house or chamber for the dead.

**Common substrates:** stone, brick, concrete, plaster, sarcophagus, body, ash, inscription.

**Symbolic operations:** house of the dead, civic memory, family continuity, royal authority, resurrection architecture.

**Examples to source later:** Roman tombs, royal mausolea, saint tombs, pyramid/tomb complexes.

**Boundary:** monumental tomb form does not prove hidden-school continuity.

### Sarcophagus

**Function:** container for the body; often also a sculptural and narrative form.

**Common substrates:** stone, marble, wood, lead, inscription, relief.

**Symbolic operations:** containment, body-image, narrative afterlife, substitution, resurrection.

**Examples to source later:** Roman columned sarcophagi, Christian sarcophagi, Egyptian coffins.

**Boundary:** carved architectural forms on a sarcophagus do not imply the same function as a full building.

### Urn / cinerary vessel

**Function:** container for cremated remains.

**Common substrates:** ash, bone fragments, ceramic, metal, glass, stone.

**Symbolic operations:** reduced body, essence, containment, portable remains, memory vessel.

**Examples to source later:** Roman cinerary urns, columbarium niches, modern cremation urns.

**Boundary:** ash containment does not prove full-body entombment.

### Reliquary

**Function:** container or display object for relics.

**Common substrates:** bone, hair, blood, cloth, metal, glass, enamel, gems, wood.

**Symbolic operations:** relic-core, sanctified containment, presence, pilgrimage focus, body-as-authority.

**Examples to source later:** reliquary busts, arm reliquaries, altar reliquaries, catacomb saints.

**Boundary:** relic presence does not prove relic matter in construction materials.

### Ossuary

**Function:** collective or secondary bone storage; sometimes architectural or decorative.

**Common substrates:** human bones, skulls, stone, wood, chapel architecture.

**Symbolic operations:** death-as-architecture, memento mori, collective remains, ancestor accumulation.

**Examples to source later:** Sedlec Ossuary, Capuchin Crypt, catacombs.

**Boundary:** ossuary evidence supports bone architecture only for specific sites.

## 2. Vertical and axial forms

### Column

**Function:** support, monument, marker, axis, victory sign, civic memory object.

**Common substrates:** stone, marble, brick, concrete, metal, inscription, base, capital.

**Symbolic operations:** axis, ascent, support, apotheosis, public memory, grave-as-axis in funerary contexts.

**Examples to source later:** Trajan's Column, Capitol Columns, column-tomb traditions.

**Boundary:** column form does not imply human remains inside the shaft.

### Pillar / stele / cippus

**Function:** upright marker, grave sign, inscription carrier, boundary stone, memorial post.

**Common substrates:** stone, inscription, relief, ash cavity in some funerary object classes.

**Symbolic operations:** name made vertical, boundary, grave marker, public memory, legal marker.

**Examples to source later:** Roman cippi, Greek stelae, Egyptian stelae, boundary stones.

**Boundary:** marker function must be separated from container function.

### Obelisk

**Function:** vertical solar or memorial monument; often transported/reused across contexts.

**Common substrates:** stone, inscription, civic relocation, spolia.

**Symbolic operations:** solar axis, state power, conquest, relocation of sacred/civic authority.

**Examples to source later:** Egyptian obelisks in Rome, modern civic obelisks.

**Boundary:** later relocation changes reception and does not preserve all original meanings.

### Tower / spire

**Function:** vertical aspiration, city marker, sacred height, watch, bell support.

**Common substrates:** stone, brick, wood, metal, bells, clockwork.

**Symbolic operations:** ascent, surveillance, call, civic/sacred authority, body extended upward.

**Examples to source later:** church spires, bell towers, minarets, civic towers.

**Boundary:** verticality alone does not prove funerary meaning.

## 3. Threshold and passage forms

### Gate / doorway / portal

**Function:** controlled passage between zones.

**Common substrates:** stone, wood, metal, inscription, sculpture.

**Symbolic operations:** threshold, initiation, admission, exclusion, death/rebirth passage.

**Examples to source later:** cathedral portals, tomb doors, lodge thresholds, temple gates.

**Boundary:** passage symbolism requires context; not every doorway is initiatory.

### Arch / vault

**Function:** span, support, passage, enclosure.

**Common substrates:** stone, brick, mortar, concrete, plaster.

**Symbolic operations:** burden transformed into form, compression stabilized, threshold, heaven/crypt enclosure.

**Examples to source later:** Roman arches, crypt vaults, cathedral vaults, bridge arches.

**Boundary:** engineering form may carry symbolic reading but is not proof of symbolic intention.

### Crypt / undercroft / cave

**Function:** underground or lower chamber; burial, support, memory, descent.

**Common substrates:** stone, columns, vaults, tombs, relics, foundation.

**Symbolic operations:** descent, hidden body, underworld, root, support, death-axis.

**Examples to source later:** Capitol Crypt, church crypts, catacombs, cave shrines.

**Boundary:** crypt-like form does not mean actual burial occurred.

### Labyrinth / path / garden itinerary

**Function:** controlled movement through symbolic or ritual space.

**Common substrates:** pavement, hedge, stone, inscription, fountain, architecture.

**Symbolic operations:** initiation, wandering, descent/ascent, purification, courtship, revelation.

**Examples to source later:** cathedral labyrinths, Hypnerotomachia gardens, sacred gardens.

**Boundary:** symbolic path readings need source or contextual support.

## 4. Light and image forms

### Window / rose window / stained glass

**Function:** admits, colors, and organizes light.

**Common substrates:** glass, metal colorants, lead, stone tracery, paint, stain.

**Symbolic operations:** light-body, celestial order, halo/aureole extension, revelation, colored doctrine.

**Examples to source later:** Chartres, Sainte-Chapelle, Hall nimbus/aureole passage.

**Boundary:** color symbolism does not prove organ-derived colorant.

### Halo / aureole / mandorla

**Function:** visualizes sanctity, radiance, body-of-light, sacred presence.

**Common substrates:** gold leaf, pigment, glass, fresco, mosaic, manuscript paint.

**Symbolic operations:** light-body, apotheosis, sanctity, spiritual sun, body transformed.

**Examples to source later:** Christian icons, manuscript illumination, Hall's nimbus/aureole reading.

**Boundary:** iconographic light does not establish material composition.

### Icon / fresco / mural

**Function:** sacred or public image on prepared surface.

**Common substrates:** plaster, pigment, binder, wall, panel, gold, lime.

**Symbolic operations:** presence, memory, doctrine, body-image, wall-as-skin.

**Examples to source later:** fresco cycles, icons, funerary murals, catacomb painting.

**Boundary:** image meaning and material binder must be evidenced separately.

### Mosaic / tessellation

**Function:** image built from fragments.

**Common substrates:** stone, glass, ceramic, mortar, tesserae.

**Symbolic operations:** fragments into image, death-bind, light-body, ordered multiplicity.

**Examples to source later:** Byzantine mosaics, Roman floors, church apses.

**Boundary:** fragment assembly is not automatically esoteric.

## 5. Text and inscription forms

### Manuscript / codex / scroll

**Function:** transmits text, law, doctrine, image, memory.

**Common substrates:** parchment, vellum, paper, ink, pigment, gold, binding.

**Symbolic operations:** inscribed skin, word-body, transmission, authority.

**Examples to source later:** illuminated manuscripts, Torah scrolls, charters, prayer books.

**Boundary:** parchment's animal origin does not imply human remains or hidden ritual.

### Epitaph / inscription / tablet

**Function:** fixes name, law, dedication, memory, boundary, ownership.

**Common substrates:** stone, bronze, clay, wax, wood, plaster, paper.

**Symbolic operations:** name-binding, public memory, legal presence, identity survival.

**Examples to source later:** Roman epitaphs, tomb inscriptions, boundary stones, votive tablets.

**Boundary:** inscription tells what it says, not every hidden motive behind it.

### Seal / token / medal / ring

**Function:** portable authority mark; identity, office, oath, memory.

**Common substrates:** metal, wax, gem, bone, ivory, glass, clay.

**Symbolic operations:** compression of authority, touch, oath, covenant, identity token.

**Examples to source later:** signet rings, relic medals, seals, mourning tokens.

**Boundary:** emblem interpretation needs iconographic context.

## 6. Wall, road, and built-surface forms

### Wall

**Function:** enclosure, support, boundary, surface, memory carrier.

**Common substrates:** brick, stone, mortar, plaster, paint, salt, moisture.

**Symbolic operations:** body, boundary, death-bind, weeping wall, inscription surface.

**Examples to source later:** brick walls, cemetery walls, cathedral walls, city walls, fresco walls.

**Boundary:** wall decay is not automatically intentional symbolism.

### Brick / tile / paving

**Function:** modular fired-earth unit; surface, road, wall, floor, tomb, kiln.

**Common substrates:** clay, sand, temper, mineral colorants, kiln atmosphere.

**Symbolic operations:** royal furnace, modular body, blood-earth motif, labor memory.

**Examples to source later:** Roman brick, yellow brick, red brick, kiln sites, brick roads.

**Boundary:** brick color requires material explanation before symbolic or hard claims.

### Mortar joint / plaster surface

**Function:** binding, smoothing, covering, repair, image support.

**Common substrates:** lime, sand, water, ash, crushed brick, gypsum, pigment, salt.

**Symbolic operations:** death-bind, repair, sacrificial layer, wall-skin, salt confession.

**Examples to source later:** lime mortar joint, repointing, fresco plaster, efflorescent wall.

**Boundary:** symbolic death-bind does not prove etymology or ritual intention.

### Road / causeway / bridge

**Function:** passage, procession, trade, military movement, pilgrimage, boundary crossing.

**Common substrates:** stone, brick, gravel, mortar, timber, iron.

**Symbolic operations:** path, threshold, civic body, procession, conquest, pilgrimage.

**Examples to source later:** Roman roads, funerary roads, processional ways, bridge rituals.

**Boundary:** path symbolism requires context.

## 7. Sound, mask, and body-extension forms

### Drum

**Function:** stretched skin made resonant.

**Common substrates:** hide, wood, glue, cord, pigment.

**Symbolic operations:** sounding remains, body made voice, ritual call, ancestor sound.

**Examples to source later:** ritual drums, military drums, shamanic drums.

**Boundary:** not every drum has funerary function.

### Flute / horn / string instrument

**Function:** hollowed, stretched, or shaped matter made to sound.

**Common substrates:** bone, horn, gut, wood, metal.

**Symbolic operations:** sounding remains, breath through dead matter, transformed body as voice.

**Examples to source later:** bone flutes, gut strings, horns, bells.

**Boundary:** functional acoustic material is not automatically ritual evidence.

### Mask / garment / veil

**Function:** transforms identity, covers body, marks role, mediates presence.

**Common substrates:** wood, fiber, hide, pigment, hair, shell, metal.

**Symbolic operations:** substitution, possession, concealment, revelation, initiation.

**Examples to source later:** funerary masks, ritual masks, burial shrouds, veils.

**Boundary:** identity transformation must be tied to ritual or performance context.

## 8. Ruin and restoration forms

### Spolia / reused fragment

**Function:** old matter inserted into new order.

**Common substrates:** columns, capitals, inscriptions, tomb stones, carved blocks.

**Symbolic operations:** ruin-as-body, conquest, continuity, resurrection, memory transfer.

**Examples to source later:** reused Roman columns, relocated Capitol Columns, embedded inscriptions.

**Boundary:** reuse changes meaning; original and later meanings must be separated.

### Restored surface / patched joint

**Function:** repair and stabilize damaged artifact.

**Common substrates:** replacement stone, new mortar, consolidant, plaster, paint, resin.

**Symbolic operations:** scar, repair, resurrection, forensic record, ethical intervention.

**Examples to source later:** repointing, cathedral restoration, fresco conservation.

**Boundary:** restoration is evidence-bearing but not neutral.

### Museum fragment / civic relic

**Function:** object removed from original context and displayed as memory or heritage.

**Common substrates:** fragment, label, mount, case, landscape installation.

**Symbolic operations:** civic relic, displaced body, memory object, authority through display.

**Examples to source later:** Capitol Columns, museum sarcophagus fragments, architectural salvage.

**Boundary:** display context is later reception, not necessarily original function.

## Use rule

Forms are teaching frames, not proof by themselves.

A form shows how transformed matter becomes culturally legible. Claims about material contents, ritual intention, or hidden doctrine require sources and artifact-specific evidence.
