# Layer Model

Mortartium uses a layered reading model. The purpose is to prevent one kind of evidence from being forced to answer every question.

An object can be materially ordinary, symbolically dense, ritually important, institutionally powerful, and historically reinterpreted at the same time.

## Rule

Do not collapse layers.

A claim may be true at one layer and unproven, false, or irrelevant at another.

## The eight working layers

### 1. Rite layer

Question: what practice of death, transition, consecration, repair, or memory surrounds the object?

Examples:

- Cremation around an urn or funerary altar.
- Burial beneath a tomb marker.
- Relic translation into a shrine.
- Consecration of an altar.
- Repointing or restoration of a wall.
- Civic relocation of architectural fragments.

Failure mode: treating an object as only material when its social function depends on rite.

### 2. Substrate layer

Question: what matter is present or transformed?

Examples:

- Bone, ash, skin, hair, blood, tooth, organ, relic.
- Clay, lime, sand, glass, salt, metal, pigment, mortar.
- Parchment, hide glue, horn, shell, ivory, wood, textile.
- Ruin, spolia, broken stone, displaced architectural fabric.

Failure mode: treating every calcium, phosphate, ash, or red color as evidence of human remains.

### 3. Process layer

Question: what destructive transformation or craft sequence produced the medium?

Examples:

- Calcination and slaking in lime mortar.
- Firing in brick and ceramic.
- Vitrification in glass.
- Scraping and stretching in parchment.
- Cremation in cinerary rites.
- Grinding in pigment preparation.

Failure mode: treating destructive transformation as automatically ritual or esoteric.

### 4. Medium layer

Question: what art or craft medium carries the transformed matter?

Examples:

- Mortar joint.
- Brick wall.
- Parchment page.
- Stained glass panel.
- Fresco layer.
- Reliquary.
- Column.
- Cippus.
- Drum skin.

Failure mode: treating different media as interchangeable because they share a motif.

### 5. Form layer

Question: what stable form organizes the medium?

Examples:

- Tomb.
- Column.
- Altar.
- Crypt.
- Window.
- Manuscript.
- Ossuary.
- Wall.
- Road.
- Ruin.

Failure mode: ignoring that form changes meaning. A bone in a grave, reliquary, pigment, instrument, or ossuary wall is not the same cultural object.

### 6. Symbolic operation layer

Question: what symbolic action does the artifact perform?

Examples:

- Death-bind.
- Light-body.
- Grave-as-axis.
- Blood-earth.
- Inscribed skin.
- Relic-core.
- Royal furnace.
- Salt confession.
- Threshold descent.

Failure mode: treating symbolic coherence as material proof.

### 7. Institution and authority layer

Question: who authorizes, controls, teaches, preserves, or interprets the artifact?

Examples:

- Family.
- Church.
- Temple.
- Guild.
- State.
- Mason.
- Conservator.
- Museum.
- Esoteric order.
- Local community.

Failure mode: treating anonymous or later interpretation as original authority.

### 8. Reception and restoration layer

Question: what happens to the object after its original creation?

Examples:

- Relocation.
- Reburial.
- Translation of relics.
- Restoration.
- Repointing.
- Museum display.
- National memory.
- Esoteric rereading.
- Hoax, myth, or legend formation.

Failure mode: mistaking later reception for original intention, or treating restoration as neutral.

## How to use the model

For every example, ask the questions in order:

1. What rite or practice field surrounds it?
2. What substrate is present?
3. What destructive process transformed it?
4. What medium carries it?
5. What form organizes it?
6. What symbolic operation does it perform?
7. Who authorizes or interprets it?
8. How was it later received, restored, moved, or mythologized?
9. What evidence supports each layer?

## Worked example: brick blood motif

The motif is not a claim. The layer model separates what can be investigated.

| Layer | Question | Example answer |
|---|---|---|
| Rite | Is there a sacrifice, labor, kiln, construction, or funerary practice? | Unknown; possible ritual, labor, or esoteric context. |
| Substrate | What material is involved? | Clay, sand, iron, lime, water, kiln atmosphere; possible blood motif under test. |
| Process | What transformation occurs? | Mixing, molding, drying, firing, oxidation/reduction. |
| Medium | What medium results? | Brick. |
| Form | Where does it go? | Wall, road, kiln, temple, prison, factory, grave, ruin. |
| Symbolic operation | What might it mean? | Blood-earth, royal furnace, forced transformation. |
| Authority | Who controls it? | Brickmaker, mason, state, patron, forced labor regime, esoteric author. |
| Reception/restoration | How is it remembered? | Horror motif, legend, labor memory, restoration science. |
| Evidence boundary | What is not yet known? | No claim of blood in bricks without source or object-specific evidence. |

## Teaching purpose

The layer model allows Mortartium to teach examples without flattening them.

A reader should learn not only what an artifact is, but how to ask better questions about it.
