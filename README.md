# Mortartium

**An atlas of remains, masonry, inscription, and destructive transformation in sacred material culture.**

Mortartium is a public research project for studying artifacts in which matter is broken, burned, dissolved, ground, bound, inscribed, illuminated, or consecrated into durable cultural form.

The project reads masonry, mortar, stained glass, relics, bone ash, parchment, pigment, salt, ruins, instruments, and restoration science as layered evidence objects: material, technical, symbolic, ritual, institutional, and historical.

## Core thesis

Art and sacred craft often begin with destructive transformation. Matter is quarried, burned, slaked, fired, stretched, scraped, ground, dissolved, cut, bound, polished, illuminated, or consecrated. The resulting artifact is not merely an object. It is a stabilized remainder: a technical body, a symbolic carrier, an institutional claim, and a historical witness.

Mortartium investigates this pattern across the royal arts, operative masonry, sacred architecture, relic culture, manuscript culture, pigment technology, glass, ceramic, restoration science, and mystery-school symbolism.

## Why “Mortartium”

The name is constructed from several overlapping registers:

- **mort-**: death, mortality, mortification, remains.
- **art**: craft, image, making, royal art.
- **mortarium**: the Latin vessel or trough where substances are crushed, mixed, and transformed; the source lineage for “mortar.”
- **-ium**: a place, medium, chamber, or field of operation.

Mortartium names the chamber where remains become art: the vessel in which dead, broken, burned, or transformed matter is recomposed into architecture, inscription, image, sound, memory, doctrine, or authority.

## Research posture

Mortartium is neither a conspiracy archive nor a purely symbolic essay project. It is a claim-disciplined research atlas.

Material evidence comes first. Symbolic interpretation comes second. Extraordinary claims remain testable and object-specific.

The project distinguishes:

- **Established claims**: ordinary documented craft, conservation, historical, or material facts.
- **Strongly supported claims**: claims supported by multiple independent evidence types.
- **Plausible claims**: claims supported by partial evidence, analogy, or a bounded interpretive tradition.
- **Speculative claims**: symbolically coherent but not materially proven.
- **Open tests**: concrete questions that could be resolved by conservation science, archive work, object study, or field documentation.
- **Rejected claims**: claims contradicted by evidence or too broad to responsibly maintain.

## Primary research layers

Each artifact is read across multiple layers. Most important artifacts are not single-layer objects.

1. **Material substrate** — bone, ash, skin, parchment, lime, brick, stone, glass, pigment, salt, metal, shell, horn, wood, textile, glue, blood, collagen, casein, resin.
2. **Destructive transformation** — calcination, slaking, firing, vitrification, pulverization, dissolution, fermentation, scraping, stretching, carving, cutting, binding, polishing, illumination.
3. **Constructive artifact form** — wall, mortar joint, arch, vault, window, halo, altar, crypt, reliquary, manuscript, fresco, pigment layer, instrument, textile, seal, cornerstone, garden, lodge room, ruin.
4. **Symbolic operation** — death-bind, purification, resurrection, illumination, inscription, containment, threshold, ascent, descent, sacrifice, covenant, secrecy, confession, reconciliation.
5. **Institutional authority** — mason, priest, monk, scribe, alchemist, guild, royal patron, mystery school, temple workshop, cathedral chapter, conservator, scientist, museum, archive, state, lineage.
6. **Evidence type** — recipe, workshop manual, conservation report, XRF, Raman, FTIR, SEM-EDS, petrography, radiocarbon, isotopic analysis, pigment analysis, mortar thin section, glass composition, relic inventory, field photograph, restoration log, initiatic text.
7. **Claim grade** — established, strongly supported, plausible, speculative, open test, rejected.

## Initial archetypes

Mortartium begins with ten working archetypes.

### 1. Death-Bind

Mortar, lime, glue, resin, plaster, cement, oath, and joint. This archetype studies binding as the transformation of fragments into body: brick to wall, stone to temple, ash to plaster, skin to codex, oath to order.

### 2. Weeping Wall

Efflorescence, lime bloom, salt crust, damp stain, calthemite, sulfate attack, spalling, iron staining, copper staining. The artifact externalizes hidden chemistry through its surface.

### 3. Light-Body

Stained glass, halo, aureole, rose window, vesica, icon, illuminated manuscript. Matter becomes a carrier of light, sanctity, celestial order, or visionary body.

### 4. Relic-Core

Altars, reliquaries, crypts, saint bodies, bone chapels, tombs, catacombs, foundation deposits. The dead body or contact relic anchors sacred space and institutional memory.

### 5. Inscribed Skin

Parchment, vellum, leather, manuscript, codex, scroll, legal charter, sacred text. Skin becomes text; flesh becomes durable law, memory, doctrine, or transmission surface.

### 6. Bone-to-Color

Bone black, bone ash, ivory, cremains ceramic, bone china, phosphate pigments. Skeleton becomes pigment, vessel, whiteness, blackness, memorial medium, or symbolic substrate.

### 7. Royal Furnace

Kilns, forges, lime kilns, glass furnaces, alchemical vessels. Controlled fire destroys and purifies matter into brick, ceramic, glass, metal, lime, ash, or pigment.

### 8. Threshold / Initiation

Arches, doorways, veils, gates, crypt descents, lodge rooms, labyrinths, garden paths. Architecture becomes passage through symbolic death, secrecy, admission, and rebirth.

### 9. Ruin-as-Body

Ruins, fragments, spolia, restored surfaces, patched masonry, weathered inscriptions. Civilization becomes corpse, fragment becomes witness, restoration becomes disciplined resurrection.

### 10. Sounding Remains

Drums, gut strings, bone flutes, horn instruments, bells, hollowed wood. Dead or transformed matter becomes voice, vibration, invocation, mourning, or ritual sound.

## Initial target classes

- Historic lime mortar, brickwork, repointing, sulfate attack, carbonation, spalling, and efflorescence.
- Cathedral glass, rose windows, halos, aureoles, vesica forms, icon pigment, and light-symbolism.
- Relics, crypts, ossuaries, reliquaries, altar stones, saint bodies, catacomb saints, and bone chapels.
- Parchment, vellum, hide glue, gelatin, collagen, casein, egg, and proteinaceous binders.
- Bone black, bone ash, cremains art, bone china, ivory, shell, horn, and tooth.
- Alchemical and Masonic symbolic corpora: royal art, salt/mercury/sulfur, rough ashlar, perfect ashlar, death-rebirth, Hiram, temple-body symbolism.
- Restoration science: conservation reports, material analysis, repair ethics, provenance, and the evidence boundary between original fabric and later intervention.

## Initial real-world sites and object fields

- Chartres Cathedral — stained glass, sacred geometry, Marian light-symbolism.
- Sainte-Chapelle — glass architecture organized around Passion relics.
- Notre-Dame de Paris — restoration science, stone, lead, timber, glass, and fire aftermath.
- Rosslyn Chapel — symbolic masonry, stone carving, restoration, and speculative reception history.
- Hagia Sophia — crushed brick/lime mortars, Byzantine material science, imperial sacred architecture.
- Assisi Basilica — fresco, restoration, saint-centered architecture.
- Sedlec Ossuary and Capuchin Crypt — explicit bone architecture and devotional remains.
- Westminster Abbey, Canterbury, York Minster — English tomb, glass, masonry, restoration, and institutional memory.
- Manuscript collections — parchment, vellum, illumination, sacred text on prepared skin.
- Instrument traditions — skin drums, gut strings, bone flutes, horns, bells, and ritual sound.

## Hard questions

Mortartium will not assume extraordinary material claims without object-specific evidence. The project will explicitly test questions such as:

- Were human or animal remains ever incorporated into specific sacred architectural media?
- Where do bone ash, phosphate, apatite, blood, collagen, animal glue, casein, egg, or other organic residues appear in conservation analysis?
- What separates ordinary workshop materials from relic matter, consecrated matter, memorial matter, or symbolic interpretation?
- How do restoration records distinguish original fabric, later repair, contamination, environmental decay, and ritual addition?
- How do operative craft processes become speculative or initiatic doctrines?

## Claim boundary

The broad thesis is expected to be strongly supportable: many sacred and fine arts convert destroyed, dead, broken, or transformed matter into durable symbolic form.

The masonry thesis is expected to be strongly supportable: lime, mortar, brick, salt migration, carbonation, spalling, and restoration practice materially enact destructive transformation, binding, decay, and repair.

The remains-in-art thesis is expected to be strongly supportable: bone, skin, glue, ash, ivory, relics, ossuaries, parchment, bone black, and memorial media are widespread across art and sacred material culture.

The strongest stained-glass or organ-specific claims are not assumed. They remain object-specific open tests until supported by conservation chemistry, recipe evidence, archival records, or traceable material analysis.
